Yes. I found quite a bit of historical information on PeCo75, and it is an interesting instrument—particularly because it appears to be a direct predecessor of the Wagtech Digital Arsenator.

What PeCo75 was

The PeCo75 was a small, battery-operated field photometer for measuring arsenic in water, developed in Austria by Prof. Walter Kosmus at the University of Graz and associated with Peters Engineering. Contemporary documents describe it as a miniaturized/handheld version of the earlier Arsenator. IRC Wash+1

Its basic analytical chemistry was the Gutzeit arsine-generation method, but with an important modification:

  1. Arsenic in the water is converted to arsine (AsH₃).

  2. The arsine passes through a filter/paper containing mercuric bromide.

  3. The paper develops a colored stain.

  4. Instead of merely comparing the stain visually with a color chart, PeCo75 puts the paper into a small photometer.

  5. The instrument measures the color intensity and displays an arsenic concentration on an LCD.

One contemporary description specifically says that it replaced zinc with sodium borohydride, avoiding the problem of obtaining zinc with sufficiently low arsenic contamination. studylib.net+1

Relationship to the Arsenator

The history seems to be approximately:

Arsenator 510 → PeCo75 → Wagtech Digital Arsenator

A 2001 field study explicitly describes PeCo75 as the replacement for the Arsenator 510 and says it used a photometer developed by Kosmus and produced by Peters Engineering. IRC Wash

Then, in the early 2000s, Kosmus and Wagtech entered into an arrangement to commercialize the technology. A contemporary report says PeCo75 was being redesigned to make it more user-friendly and to provide a digital readout down to about 2 ppb. Scribd

That development appears to have become the Wagtech Digital Arsenator. A later scientific paper explicitly calls PeCo75 a precursor to Arsenator. ScienceDirect

Some surprisingly useful technical information

I found an EPA Environmental Technology Verification report for the Peters Engineering AS75, which appears to be extremely closely related to the PeCo75 system. This is probably one of the best surviving technical descriptions.

The AS75 consisted of:

  • the PeCo chemical test apparatus

  • an electronic photometer

  • 100-mL reaction flask

  • two filter holders

  • 50-, 10-, and 1-mL pipettes

  • filters

  • reagents in tablet form

  • color charts as a backup

The electronic reader itself was:

  • 120 × 60 × 26 mm

  • 160 g including a 9-V battery

  • 1-ppb display resolution

  • approximately 260 hours of battery life

  • up to about 1,200 measurements per battery. EPA Nepis

The two filter holders provided different concentration ranges. The color charts covered approximately:

  • gray holder: 10, 20, 30 … 100 ppb

  • blue holder: 2.5, 5, 10, 20 … 60 ppb

So this wasn't simply a crude "arsenic present/not present" kit. It was intended to provide a numerical measurement.

The reaction itself took roughly 15 minutes according to one description of PeCo75. ResearchGate

How good was it?

This is particularly interesting for your arsenic work.

A major Bangladesh field program used PeCo75 extensively. In one quality-control program, teams periodically analyzed 50 and 250 µg/L arsenic standards to monitor the instrument. IRC Wash

A later review of those results calculated:

As concentrationRepeatability SD
50 µg/L8.4 µg/L
300 µg/L43.7 µg/L

The resulting error model was substantially better than some conventional visual field kits. NERC Open Research Archive

There is an important caveat, though: the absolute accuracy wasn't necessarily as good as the digital resolution suggests. A later evaluation of the successor Wagtech Arsenator found that calibration against laboratory methods was important, and concluded that the instrument was suitable for field screening, particularly around 5–100 µg/L. ScienceDirect

So I would regard a reading such as 7 ppb as very different from saying that the instrument can reliably quantify arsenic to 1 ppb.

Why PeCo75 is particularly interesting

There are actually two things worth separating here.

1. The chemistry

The PeCo system is basically a clever, field-hardened arsine-generation + mercury-bromide detection system.

That means its detection chemistry is fundamentally quite old, but it gets a lot of sensitivity out of a very small amount of equipment.

2. The optical reader

The really interesting part from an instrumentation standpoint is the tiny photometer.

The original visual Gutzeit test asks:

"How dark is this spot compared with these reference spots?"

PeCo75 instead asks:

"How much light is reflected from this spot?"

The source I found describing the evolution of the instruments makes an explicit distinction: the original Arsenator used a light-through approach, whereas PeCo75 used reflected-light measurement. It says that this made it possible to put the instrument into something roughly the size of a pocket calculator. NERC Open Research Archive

That could be very relevant if you're thinking about modernizing or reproducing the concept.

One particularly useful historical source

The most valuable document I found so far is the EPA/Battelle ETV verification of the AS75. It gives unusually concrete information about the physical instrument, chemistry, sample volumes, filters, calibration, accuracy and field operation. EPA Nepis

EPA/Battelle AS75 verification report

There is also a substantial WaterAid/DFID/WS Atkins report documenting actual PeCo75 use in Bangladesh, including QC procedures. IRC Wash+1
